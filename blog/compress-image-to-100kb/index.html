How to compress an image to 100 KB, 50 KB or 20 KB

By TinyWow Now team · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026 · 6 min read

To get a photo under 100 KB, crop it and reduce its width to about 1,000 to 1,200 pixels. Then save it as a JPG at roughly 70 to 80 percent quality. Cutting pixels does most of the work, and lowering quality alone rarely shrinks a phone photo that far without visible damage. The Compress Image tool on TinyWow Now does this for you. Type a target size such as 100 KB, and the tool finds the best quality that fits. It runs in your browser, so your photo never uploads.

Why the quality slider alone is not enough

A JPG stores colour information for the pixels in the picture, so the more pixels it has, the bigger the file. A typical phone photo is 4000 by 3000 pixels, which is 12,000,000 pixels, and files like that are commonly several megabytes. A version 1200 pixels wide (1200 by 900) has about 1,080,000 pixels, roughly eleven times fewer. At 800 by 600 it has 480,000, twenty-five times fewer.

That is why the reliable order is: crop, then shrink the pixel size, then lower the quality. If you only drag the quality down, you end up with a full-size picture that is full of blocky artefacts and still too heavy.

Step by step: get an image under your size limit

  1. Read the rules on the form. Note the maximum file size and the pixel size, if the form gives one. Note the formats it accepts (usually JPG) and whether it sets a minimum size too.
  2. Crop first. Open Crop Image and remove everything you do not need. For a passport-style photo, keep the head and shoulders. For a signature, crop tightly around the ink. Less area means fewer pixels to store.
  3. Let the tool hit your limit. Open Compress Image, drop your file and type your limit in Target size, or tap the 20, 50, 100 or 200 KB button. The tool finds the highest quality that fits under that size and shrinks the pixel size only when it has to. It counts 1 KB as 1,000 bytes, so the result also fits under forms that count 1,024.
  4. Check the result. Under Compressed you can see the new size next to the original. If the tool marks a file as Over target, crop it tighter or choose a slightly larger target.
  5. Prefer to control it yourself? Leave Target size empty, open Advanced options, set Max width from the table below, choose JPG as the output format and start the quality at 80%. Lower the quality by 5 at a time. Below about 65%, reduce the max width instead, because quality that low shows artefacts around edges and text.

The final size depends on the picture: a photo with a plain wall behind the subject compresses far more than a crowded street scene. Always read the size shown after compression before you upload.

Signatures and photos with text

Lossy JPG compression struggles with sharp edges and text, because it smears them. web.dev warns that high-contrast text and line art are prone to artefacts. For a signature, crop tightly, keep the pixel size the form asks for, and avoid low quality. Signature fields are often stricter than photo fields, so check whether yours says 10 KB or 20 KB. If the form accepts PNG, a clean scan on a plain white background can sometimes shrink more as a PNG than as a JPG. Compare both.

Common mistakes that make the file bigger or worse

  • Compressing the same file again and again. Each lossy pass discards more detail. Go back to your original and make one careful pass.
  • Using PNG for a photo. PNG is lossless, so the quality slider has no effect on it and photos stay large. Switch the output to JPG or WebP.
  • Enlarging a small image. Making it bigger adds pixels but no detail, and the file grows.
  • Going under the minimum. Some forms reject images that are too small in pixels or too light in size. Check both limits.

Is it safe to compress a passport photo online?

It depends on the tool. Many online compressors upload your file to a server, which matters for ID photos and signatures. A browser-based tool processes the file on your own device instead. TinyWow Now works this way, and you can check it yourself instead of taking our word for it:

  1. Open the Compress Image page and press F12 to open your browser's developer tools.
  2. Go to the Network tab and clear it.
  3. Compress a photo. You should see no request that carries your image to another server.

We ran this check ourselves on 21 November 2026: compressing a test image (261 KB down to 137 KB) made no network requests.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compress an image to 100 KB without losing quality?

Strictly speaking, JPG compression always discards some data, so nothing at that size is truly lossless. You can avoid visible loss by cropping, reducing the width to what the form needs and keeping the quality around 75 to 80 percent. At normal viewing size most photos look the same as the original.

Why is my image still over the limit after compressing?

Usually because the pixel size is still too large. Reduce the Max width first, then fine-tune the quality. Also check that the output format is JPG, since PNG does not shrink with the quality slider.

Can I set an exact target size like 100 KB?

Yes. Type it in the Target size box on Compress Image, or tap 20, 50, 100 or 200 KB. The tool finds the highest quality that fits and reduces the pixel size only if it has to. When you set a target, the tool saves PNG images as JPG. We tested targets of 200, 100, 50 and 20 KB on a large 4000 by 3000 test image, and every result came in under its target.

Will a 50 KB image look blurry?

On screen at normal size, a photo of about 800 pixels wide at 70 percent quality usually looks fine. Fine text, thin lines and large print sizes are where the loss shows.

What is the difference between KB and MB?

A megabyte is about 1,000 kilobytes, or 1,024 in some systems. A 2 MB photo is therefore roughly 2,000 KB, and it has to lose about 95 percent of its size to fit under 100 KB.

Does the tool upload my image when I compress it here?

No. The compression runs in your browser, and you can confirm that in the Network tab of your developer tools, as described above.
